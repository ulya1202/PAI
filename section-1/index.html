  • ▶🎲 What is probability?click me!

    [[prob|Probability]] is a number from 0 to 1 that says how likely something is. 0 = it never happens, 1 = it always happens.

    Three words we use all the time:

    • [[exp|Experiment]]: something random we do. Roll a die.
    • [[ss|Sample space S]]: all possible outcomes. S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}
    • [[event|Event E]]: some of the outcomes, the ones we care about. E = "the die is even" = {2, 4, 6}

    If all outcomes are equally likely, we just count:

    P(E) = |E| / |S| = (outcomes in E) / (all outcomes)
    Example: P(even) = 3 / 6 = 0.5
    🔁 Another way to see it: repeat the experiment many, many times. P(E) is the fraction of times E happens. Try it on the right!
    🐍 Python code: probability by counting and by repeating
    import random

S = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]
E = [2, 4, 6]
print(len(E) / len(S))                  # 0.5 (by counting)

hits = 0
for i in range(10000):
    if random.choice(S) in E:
        hits += 1
print(hits / 10000)                     # about 0.5 (by repeating)

    • ▶📜 The 3 axiomsthe basic rules

      An [[axiom|axiom]] is a rule we accept without proof. All of probability is built on just these 3:

      Axiom 1: 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1
      Axiom 2: P(S) = 1
      Axiom 3: if E and F are [[me|mutually exclusive]], P(E or F) = P(E) + P(F)
      • 1: a probability is never negative and never more than 1 (no "120% chance"!).
      • 2: something from the sample space always happens.
      • 3: if two events can't happen at the same time, we can add their chances.
      🎬 Moana & Up: we ask a student which of the two movies they like. Every student is in exactly one box: only Moana, only Up, both, or neither. These 4 boxes are mutually exclusive and cover everybody. So their chances must add up to 1.
      Move the sliders on the right and see when the axioms break! 💥

      • ▶➕ OR: P(E or F)E ∪ F: at least one happens

        "E or F" ([[union|E ∪ F]]) means: E happens, or F happens, or both.

        Our first idea: just add P(E) + P(F). But be careful! If E and F overlap, the overlap is counted two times. 😬

        🎬 P(likes Moana) = 0.6, P(likes Up) = 0.5. Is P(Moana or Up) = 1.1? No! That breaks Axiom 1. The students who like both were counted twice.

        So the first question for OR is always: can E and F happen at the same time? The tree splits here 👇

        🎬 Moana or Up?
        P(Moana)
        P(Up)      
        P(both)   
        • ▶✅ Mutually exclusiveno overlap → just add

          [[me|Mutually exclusive]] = E and F can't happen at the same time. No overlap.

          P(E or F) = P(E) + P(F)

          This is Axiom 3! Example with one die: E = "1 or 2", F = "a 5". One die can't show a 2 and a 5 at once.

          P = 2/6 + 1/6 = 3/6 ✔

          💡 Works for many events too: if no two of them overlap, P(E₁ or E₂ or …) = P(E₁) + P(E₂) + …
          🎲 E = {1, 2}, F = {5}
        • ▶⚠️ NOT mutually exclusiveoverlap → subtract it once

          E and F can happen together. We add, then remove the overlap once, because we counted it twice. This is called [[ie|inclusion–exclusion]].

          P(E or F) = P(E) + P(F) − P(E and F)

          Example: E = "even" {2, 4, 6}, F = "3 or less" {1, 2, 3}. The 2 is in both!

          P = 3/6 + 3/6 − 1/6 = 5/6 ✔

          🎬 Moana or Up: 0.6 + 0.5 − 0.3 = 0.8 ✔

          🐍 Python code
          E = {2, 4, 6}
F = {1, 2, 3}
print(len(E | F) / 6)                            # 5/6 (count E ∪ F)
print(len(E)/6 + len(F)/6 - len(E & F)/6)       # 5/6 (formula)
          🎲 even OR ≤ 3
      • ▶✖️ AND: P(E and F)E ∩ F: both happen

        "E and F" ([[inter|E ∩ F]]) means: both E and F happen.

        Example: we roll two dice. The sample space has 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely outcomes. Look at the grid on the right: every cell is one outcome.

        "Die 1 is a 6 and die 2 is a 6" is just 1 cell: 1/36.

        📐 The general AND rule (the [[chain|chain rule]]):
        P(E and F) = P(E) · P(F | E)
        [[cond|P(F | E)]] = "the chance of F, if we already know E happened". This is a conditional probability.

        So the question for AND is: does knowing E change the chance of F? The tree splits here 👇

        🎲🎲 Two dice: 36 outcomes
        • ▶✅ Independentno effect → just multiply

          [[indep|Independent]] = knowing that E happened doesn't change the chance of F. So P(F | E) = P(F), and the AND rule becomes simple:

          P(E and F) = P(E) · P(F)

          Two dice don't talk to each other! P(6 and 6) = 1/6 · 1/6 = 1/36 ✔ (the same as counting cells).

          Coins, dice, and two separate calls to a language model are independent. (We need this in chapter 2! 🤖)

          💡 Works for many events: P(E₁ and E₂ and …) = P(E₁) · P(E₂) · …
          🐍 Python code
          import random
hits = 0
for i in range(36000):
    d1 = random.randint(1, 6)
    d2 = random.randint(1, 6)
    if d1 == 6 and d2 == 6:
        hits += 1
print(hits / 36000, 1/6 * 1/6)    # both about 0.0278
          🎲🎲 Roll two dice
        • ▶⚠️ NOT independentmultiplying gives a wrong answer

          Sometimes one event does change the chance of the other. Then P(E) · P(F) is wrong, and we need P(F | E).

          E = "die 1 is a 6" (6/36), F = "the sum is 11 or more" (3/36).
          Just multiply: 6/36 · 3/36 ≈ 0.014 ❌
          Count the cells: (6,5), (6,6) → 2/36 ≈ 0.056 ✔
          Why? If die 1 is a 6, a big sum is much more likely: P(F | E) = 2/6, not 3/36.
          Chain rule: 1/6 · 2/6 = 2/36 ✔

          Only multiply P(E) · P(F) if you can say why the events are independent. Conditional probability comes back later in the course.

          🔗 die 1 = 6 AND sum ≥ 11
      • ▶🔄 Complement: 1 − Pthe "lazy" trick

        The [[comp|complement]] Eᶜ means "E does NOT happen". E and Eᶜ are mutually exclusive, and together they make the whole sample space. So P(E) + P(Eᶜ) = 1 (Axioms 2 and 3!):

        P(E) = 1 − P(Eᶜ)
        😴 Why is this so useful? Very often, calculating P(E) directly is long and annoying, because it has many cases. But the opposite event has only a few cases. So it is easier to calculate the opposite and subtract it from 1.
        Example: roll n dice. What is P(at least one 6)?
        Directly: exactly one 6, or exactly two 6s, or … many cases! 😩
        Opposite: "no 6 at all" is just one case: every die is not a 6 → (5/6)ⁿ (independent → multiply!)
        So: P(at least one 6) = 1 − (5/6)ⁿ 🎉

        💡 Hear "at least one"? Think complement!

        🐍 Python code
        n = 4
print(1 - (5/6)**n)       # 0.518

import random
hits = sum(any(random.randint(1, 6) == 6 for d in range(n))
           for i in range(10000))
print(hits / 10000)       # about 0.518

  • ▶🤖 The hook: Same prompt, different answers?click me!

    Ask a chatbot the same question twice, and you often get two different answers. Is that a bug or a feature? 🤔

    • 🎨 Feature: if a model always said the same thing, asking again would be pointless. Variety is great for brainstorming and creative writing.
    • 🐞 Bug (sometimes): for a factual answer, we want the same answer every time.

    How does a model choose? It does not pick one answer. It reads the [[prompt|prompt]], gives a probability to every possible ending, and then it [[sample|samples]] one, like rolling a special die. 🎲

    Our [[toy|toy model]] reads the prompt A sleepy robot dreams about… and it can finish it in only 5 ways:

    • 🐑 "A sleepy robot dreams about electric sheep"
    • 🔌 "A sleepy robot dreams about a quiet charging station"
    • 🧩 "A sleepy robot dreams about solving puzzles"
    • 🔢 "A sleepy robot dreams about counting outcomes"
    • 🐶 "A sleepy robot dreams about robot puppies"
    🔑 Each ending has its own chance, but only for this prompt. For example, 0.44 is the chance of "…dreams about electric sheep" given that the prompt is "A sleepy robot dreams about". So every chance in the table is a conditional probability (more about this in Step 3!).
    ❓ Our question: we give the model the same prompt twice. What is the probability that the two answers are different?
    🧠 Make a gut guess first: We'll come back to it!
    🐍 Python code: the toy model
    import random

prompt  = "A sleepy robot dreams about"
endings = ["electric sheep", "a quiet charging station", "solving puzzles",
           "counting outcomes", "robot puppies"]
probs   = [0.44, 0.20, 0.14, 0.13, 0.09]   # P(ending | prompt)

def prompt_model():
    ending = random.choices(endings, weights=probs)[0]
    return prompt + " " + ending

print(prompt_model())
print(prompt_model())    # maybe different!

    👇 Open the solution, step by step.

    💤 Prompt: "A sleepy robot dreams about…"
    > A sleepy robot dreams about _
    P( ending | prompt = "A sleepy robot dreams about" )
    Bars = the chance of each ending, given this prompt. Orange = what it chose this time. Last number = how many times so far.
    • ▶🧩 The solution, step by stepthink first, then click

      For every step: read the question, think, and then click "Show the answer".

      Step 1 ❓ We prompt twice. What can happen? What does "same" mean, and what does "different" mean?
      Every result is a pair: (what call 1 says, what call 2 says).
      "Same" has only 5 pairs:
      🐑 dreams about electric sheep + 🐑 dreams about electric sheep
      🔌 dreams about a quiet charging station + 🔌 dreams about a quiet charging station
      🧩 dreams about solving puzzles + 🧩 dreams about solving puzzles
      🔢 dreams about counting outcomes + 🔢 dreams about counting outcomes
      🐶 dreams about robot puppies + 🐶 dreams about robot puppies
      "Different" has 20 pairs, for example:
      🐑 dreams about electric sheep + 🐶 dreams about robot puppies
      🐶 dreams about robot puppies + 🐑 dreams about electric sheep …
      (Careful: these two are 2 different cases! The order matters: call 1 first, call 2 second.)
      👉 20 cases is a lot of work, 5 is much less. So let's find P(same) first. All 25 pairs are in the square on the right. 👉
      Step 2 ❓ Let's name the events in words first.
      E = the event that both calls give the same ending.
      Bsheep = the event that both calls say "A sleepy robot dreams about electric sheep". In the same way: Bstation, Bpuzzles, Bcounting, Bpuppies.
      (On the slides they are called B₁ … B₅, and pᵢ is the chance of ending i.)
      Step 3 ❓ Where does the number 0.44 come from? Is it just "the chance of electric sheep"?
      Not exactly! The model doesn't choose "electric sheep" out of nothing. First it reads the prompt, and then it chooses the next words. So 0.44 is the chance of "electric sheep" GIVEN the prompt:
      psheep = P( "electric sheep" | prompt = "A sleepy robot dreams about" ) = 0.44
      This is a [[cond|conditional probability]]. Read "|" as "given".
      🔁 With a different prompt, the chances would be different! (Try it in "Wait, where do the chances come from?" below.)
      ✅ In our problem, both calls get the same prompt, so both calls use the same table of chances.
      Step 4 ❓ What is the chance that both calls say "dreams about electric sheep"? Do we add or multiply? Why?
      Say it in words: call 1 says "dreams about electric sheep" AND call 2 says "dreams about electric sheep".
      It is an AND. Both calls get the same prompt, and call 2 doesn't remember call 1. So, given the prompt, the two calls are independent, and we multiply:
      P(both "electric sheep" | prompt) = 0.44 × 0.44 = 0.1936
      The same for every ending: both "a quiet charging station" = 0.20 × 0.20 = 0.04, both "robot puppies" = 0.09 × 0.09 = 0.0081, …
      In general: P(Bᵢ) = pᵢ · pᵢ = pᵢ²
      (🤔 Why can we multiply? See "Wait, why can we multiply?" below.)
      Step 5 ❓ Now P(same). How do we put the 5 cases together? Add or multiply? Why?
      Say it in words: "same" = both "electric sheep" OR both "a quiet charging station" OR both "solving puzzles" OR both "counting outcomes" OR both "robot puppies".
      It is an OR. Can "both dream about electric sheep" and "both dream about robot puppies" happen at the same time? No! Call 1 gives only one ending. So these cases are mutually exclusive, and we add:
      P(E) = P(Bsheep) + P(Bstation) + … + P(Bpuppies) = Σ pᵢ²
      Step 6 ❓ Put in the numbers.
      🐑 both "electric sheep": 0.44 × 0.44 = 0.1936
      🔌 both "a quiet charging station": 0.20 × 0.20 = 0.0400
      🧩 both "solving puzzles": 0.14 × 0.14 = 0.0196
      🔢 both "counting outcomes": 0.13 × 0.13 = 0.0169
      🐶 both "robot puppies": 0.09 × 0.09 = 0.0081
      P(same) = 0.1936 + 0.04 + 0.0196 + 0.0169 + 0.0081 = 0.2782
      Step 7 ❓ But we wanted "different", not "same"! How do we get it? Why can we do 1 − P(same)?
      Every pair is either "same" or "different". Never both, and there is no third option. So "different" is the NOT of "same" (the complement), and the two chances add up to 1:
      P(same) + P(different) = 1
      P(different) = P(Eᶜ) = 1 − 0.2782 ≈ 0.72
      🎉 Even this tiny 5-ending model gives a different answer almost 3 times out of 4! Different answers are the expected behavior, not a glitch.

      ✅ Sanity check: is 0.72 between 0 and 1? Yes. Extreme case: if the model always said "electric sheep" (p = 1), the answer would be 1 − 1 = 0. Makes sense!

      Every cell = one pair (call 1, call 2). The area of a cell = chance × chance (AND → multiply). Orange = "same", blue = "different". Click a cell!
      👆 Click any cell to see its story.

      The whole solution in one picture: GIVEN the prompt for every chance, AND → × inside each case, OR → + between the cases, then NOT → 1 − at the end.

      • ▶🤔 Wait, where do the chances come from?GIVEN the prompt · conditional

        The chance of "…dreams about electric sheep" is not a fixed number of the world. It depends on the prompt. The model reads the prompt first, and then it gives chances to the endings.

        pᵢ = P( ending i | prompt )

        So every pᵢ is a [[cond|conditional probability]]: "the chance of this ending, given this prompt".

        👉 Change the prompt on the right: the same endings get different chances, and the answer to our question changes too!

        ✅ In our problem the prompt is the same for both calls ("Same Prompt!"). So both calls use the same table: 0.44, 0.20, 0.14, 0.13, 0.09.

      • ▶🤔 Wait, why can we multiply?AND · conditional · independent

        "Both say electric sheep" = call 1 says "A sleepy robot dreams about electric sheep" AND call 2 says "A sleepy robot dreams about electric sheep".

        The general AND rule uses a [[cond|conditional probability]] (the [[chain|chain rule]]). Here there are two "given"s: the prompt, and what call 1 said:

        P(both sheep | prompt) =
        P(call 1 = sheep | prompt) · P(call 2 = sheep | prompt, call 1 = sheep)

        The first part is 0.44. What about the second part? Every call is a fresh sample. The model doesn't remember the first call, like a die that doesn't remember the last roll. Call 2 only "sees" the prompt:

        P(call 2 = sheep | prompt, call 1 = sheep) = P(call 2 = sheep | prompt) = 0.44

        Knowing call 1 doesn't change call 2. That's what independent means. So: 0.44 × 0.44 ✔

        ⚠️ If the model did remember call 1 (for example, it sees the chat history), the second part would be different, and 0.44 × 0.44 would be wrong. Try the 🧠 button on the right!
        🔍 Call 1 said "…dreams about electric sheep". What does call 2 say?
      • ▶🤔 Wait, why can we add?OR · mutually exclusive

        "Same" = both "electric sheep" OR both "a quiet charging station" OR both "solving puzzles" OR both "counting outcomes" OR both "robot puppies".

        Can "both dream about electric sheep" and "both dream about robot puppies" happen at the same time? No! Call 1 can't say "electric sheep" and "robot puppies" at once.

        So the cases are mutually exclusive (Axiom 3), and for OR we can just add.

        On the square: the 5 orange cells don't overlap, so the total orange area = the sum of the 5 cells.

        ⚠️ Only add when the events can't happen together. Otherwise you count something twice.

      • ▶🤔 Wait, why 1 − ?NOT · complement · 5 vs. 20

        Every pair is "same" or "different". Never both, no third option. So P(same) + P(different) = 1, and P(different) = 1 − P(same).

        Why not just add the "different" pairs? We can! It is correct, just slower: there are 20 of them, like ("electric sheep", "robot puppies"), ("robot puppies", "electric sheep"), ("a quiet charging station", "solving puzzles")… "Same" has only 5. Watch the long way 👉

        💡 When the event you want has many cases, look at the opposite.
        🐍 Python code: both ways
        p = [0.44, 0.20, 0.14, 0.13, 0.09]
direct = sum(p[i] * p[j] for i in range(5) for j in range(5) if i != j)
comp   = 1 - sum(x * x for x in p)
print(direct, comp)     # 0.7218 0.7218
        🐢 The long way: all 20 "different" pairs
